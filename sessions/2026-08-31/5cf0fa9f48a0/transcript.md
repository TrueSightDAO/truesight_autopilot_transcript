6 · Variety A — CCN-51 “Ponta Verde”: history & agronomy
Origins (Ecuador, 1960s–1980s): CCN-51 (Colección Castro Naranjal 51) was bred by Ecuadorian agronomist
Homero Castro over ~12 years of crossing and selection. The “51” marks the 51st cross in the series; the clone derives
from a 3-way cross involving IMC 67 / ICS 95 / O-1 in its pedigree. First production reports date to 1965 (Finca Sofia,
Naranjal); the variety was released publicly in 1984. It was bred as a crisis response to Ecuador's witches' broom
(Moniliophthora perniciosa) and Ceratocystis wilt (mal del machete), and for high, reliable yield. Castro died in the late
1980s before patenting it, so the clone spread freely.

--- Page 6 ---
Genetics: genetic studies put CCN-51 at roughly 1.1% Nacional, 45.4% IMC, 22.2% Criollo, 21.5% Amelonado, 3.9%
Contamana, 2.5% Purús and 2.1% Marañon — essentially a forastero-dominant hybrid with a thin Nacional fraction.
Not fine-flavour or heirloom; the industry classifies it as “bulk cacao”. ~70% of Ecuadorian production and ~90% of new
plantings there over the last two decades; now spreading across Latin America and Africa.
Agronomy: yields ~700–1,100 kg/ha on farms (vs 300–400 kg/ha for Nacional) and up to 2–3 t/ha under ideal
conditions; Brazilian field trials recorded 2,143 kg/ha (year 6) and 2,955 kg/ha (year 7). Self-compatible, grows in full
sun, tolerates a wider disease load, starts producing ~2 years after planting — the traits behind year-round fruiting.
Registered among clonal cultivars (RNC/MAPA) in Brazil; common grafting choice in the Amazon (Pará) and Bahia.
“Ponta Verde”: the name used by the farmers — not an official registry denomination. Most plausibly a local/biotype
naming of CCN-51 in Pará (green-tipped pods — “ponta verde”), i.e. the grafted CCN-51 clone as known on the farm.
7 · Variety B — common / traditional Pará cacao: history & identity
Amazonian origin: Theobroma cacao is native to the Amazon basin; DNA evidence confirms an Amazonian origin
with a strong pre-Columbian trade network (Mongabay 2024; archaeological cultivation records ~3,000+ years, with
indigenous fermentation of a bitter ritual drink — the root of the word “chocolate”). Pará state carries the world's largest
diversity of cacao germplasm (Portal Amazônia) — a product of várzea soils, climate and centuries of local selection.
Traditional landraces: the “common” cacao of Pará corresponds to the forastero/Amelonado-type landraces and
named local varieties such as Pará and Parazinho (small fruit, strong regional adaptation, characteristic flavour used
in artisanal chocolate). The farmers' “cacau comum” — seed-planted, seasonal, with a fruitless period — matches the
traditional forastero/Pará-type habit.
Pará today: Brazil's leading cacao-producing state (with Rondônia and Bahia; Embrapa mapped ~121,000 ha across
Pará and Rondônia; Pará + Bahia ≈ 96% of national output). Much new Pará planting is in agroforestry systems (SAFs)
on former pasture — a model that fits the DAO's agroforestry/reforestation mission.
8 · Chemical differences (research findings)
Direct comparisons (Nacional vs CCN-51, fermented beans — Los Ríos, Ecuador, 2025): moisture, fat, caffeine
and theobromine showed no significant differences between varieties alone, but the genotype × location
interaction was highly significant for total polyphenols (p = 0.003) and theobromine (p = 0.009) — i.e. chemistry
is driven by terroir interacting with genetics. Nacional from Montalvo peaked at 130.51 mg GAE/g polyphenols and up
to 1.92% theobromine — the best functional profile of the study. Fat content did not differ significantly.

for bitterness — among the highest bitterness in the panel, with the lowest sweetness scores — versus BN 34.

Pará pulp study (CCN-51 + CEPEC 2005 blend), pulp was low-calorie (56 kcal), 20.4 °Brix soluble solids,
potassium-rich (128 mg/100 g).
